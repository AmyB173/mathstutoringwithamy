8. THE PROBLEM (grey section)
================================================================

HEADING
What Exactly Is Happening in Schools

PARAGRAPH 1
Your child enjoyed maths at primary school, even if it often felt a bit
easy. They scored well in tests, their teachers said good things, and there
was real excitement about starting secondary school and finally getting
into something new. Harder than Year 6 was simply what everyone expected.

PARAGRAPH 2
Then Year 7 started, and I imagine your child worked out fairly quickly
that a lot of it was a repeat. Column addition and subtraction, short
division, the topics they had already mastered in Year 5 or earlier. If
they are lucky their teacher hands them extension questions to hold the
boredom off, but that is a great deal to ask of one teacher with thirty
students in front of them.

BIG PULL QUOTE
If your child is the able one who already thrived in Year 6, Year 7 can
feel like being put on pause.

PARAGRAPH 3
I do understand why schools teach it this way, and the students who arrive
without secure foundations need that year. But the ones who already had
them rarely get what they need, and this is the year a lot of able children
quietly decide that maths is boring. In every other subject they are
learning something new. In maths they are going back over old ground, and
the interest drains away faster than most parents expect.

9. WHAT WE COVER
================================================================

HEADING
What We Cover

INTRO  <-- I REWROTE THIS ONE LINE, SEE MY NOTE
A lot of what we do is rooted in algebra, and the rest is chosen to build
the kind of reasoning that makes algebra land properly.

BULLET 1
Algebra. A lot of what we will do will be rooted in algebra. We will cover
solving equations, expanding and factorising, substitution and rearranging.
All skills that are essential to long term maths success.

BULLET 2
Number theory. Primes, divisibility and factor puzzles. Real mathematical
thinking with almost no prerequisites, which is exactly why it is such a
good place to start.

BULLET 3
Angle chasing and polygons. Geometric reasoning where every step has to be
justified. Students who can do this find GCSE geometry straightforward
years later.

BULLET 4
Working backwards with area and perimeter. Given the answer, find the
shape. The same Year 7 topic their class is doing, turned around so that it
genuinely requires thought.

BULLET 5
Fractions and ratio problem solving. This topic takes up a surprising
amount of the current curriculum, and so it will be another key focus for
our year.

12. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS (click to open)
================================================================

Q1. Won't they just be bored when school teaches this later?

Honestly, probably a bit, and I would rather say that than pretend
otherwise. However, hard algebra usually arrives in Year 9 or 10, and
students are asked to learn it from scratch and master the hardest
questions on it at the same time, while nine other GCSEs compete for their
attention. A child who arrives already fluent skips that stage entirely and
spends the time pushing to the top of the paper instead. Set against that,
a handful of familiar lessons in Year 8 is a small price to pay for this
longer term gain.


Q2. What if my child misses a lesson?

If they miss a lesson they will be provided with a recording of the
session.


Q3. Can we join partway through a block?

Yes, if there is a place free. You are billed pro-rata for the sessions
that are left.


Q4. Is this better than one to one tutoring?

For this particular child, often yes. An able Year 7 who is bored does not
need remedial attention, they need harder material and other children to
bounce off. If your child has actual gaps to close, one to one might be the
better route.


Q5. What if my child is not ready for it?

Then I let you know before you pay for anything. This group is not for
students who are still shoring up the basics, and pushing a child who is
not secure would do more harm than good. Schools repeat Year 6 material for
a good reason: most students genuinely need that year.


Q6. What do we need at home?

A laptop or tablet with a working microphone, and a quiet spot to sit for
the hour. Lessons run on Pencil Spaces, a shared digital classroom
including a video call, so your child writes on the same board I do. A
cheap graphics tablet or an iPad is essential for writing on the screen.
